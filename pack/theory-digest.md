## Frantz Fanon — Overview — Frantz Fanon (thematic summary) (2026) · thematic summary
Frantz Fanon — Overview Frantz Fanon (1925–1961) was a psychiatrist, philosopher, and revolutionary from Martinique whose work fused anti-colonial politics with the psychology of oppression. He is one of the core theorists of decolonization, and his writing carries a moral and emotional charge that orthodox Marxism often lacks. Life arc Fanon was born in Fort-de-France, Martinique, then a French colony. As a young man he volunteered for the Free French forces in World War II, fighting against the Nazis — an experience that sharpened his sense that the racism he met from his own French comrades was not an accident but a structure. After the war he studied medicine and psychiatry in Lyon, France. His 1952 book Black Skin, White Masks grew out of that period: a clinical and philosophical account of what racism does to the mind of the colonized. In 1953 he took a post as head of psychiatry at the Blida-Joinville hospital in Algeria, then in the middle of the Algerian war of independence. Treating both Algerian victims of French torture and French soldiers traumatized by what they had done, Fanon concluded that colonialism itself was the pathology — no therapy could cure a wound the system kept inflicting. He resigned, joined the Algerian National Liberation Front (FLN), and became one of the revolution's leading intellectuals and diplomats.

## The Wretched of the Earth — Key Concepts — Frantz Fanon (thematic summary) (1961) · thematic summary
Breaking with orthodox Marxism, Fanon argues that in colonial countries the industrial working class is small, relatively privileged, and often tied to the colonial economy. The true revolutionary class is the peasantry — the great mass of the dispossessed — along with the lumpenproletariat of the shantytowns: the uprooted, the unemployed, the people with nothing left to lose. These are the forces the colonial regime fears most, because they cannot be bought off with small concessions. For Karl: revolutions are not made by the comfortable. Look to the people the system has discarded — they are the vanguard, not the charity case. 5. The pitfalls of national consciousness Fanon's warning to newly independent nations is brutal: the national bourgeoisie — the local elites who inherit power at independence — will betray the revolution. Educated in colonial schools, hungry for the settler's villas and businesses, they do not dismantle the colonial economy; they take the colonizer's place in it. They become intermediaries for foreign capital, selling the country's resources and labor to the highest bidder while waving the national flag. The result: independence without liberation. The flag changes; the exploitation continues under native management. Fanon's demand: national consciousness must be deepened into social and political consciousness — a consciousness of the people's needs, not just of the nation's symbols. Without that deepening, independence is a shell.

## Black Skin, White Masks — Key Concepts — Frantz Fanon (thematic summary) (1952) · thematic summary
Black Skin, White Masks — Key Concepts (Frantz Fanon, 1952) Fanon's first book is a study of the psychology of colonial racism — what it does to the mind of the colonized, and how the colonized might free themselves from it. Written when Fanon was in his twenties, it is part clinical observation, part philosophy, part cry of pain and defiance. Thematic summary below. 1. Sociogeny: the social origin of the wound Fanon's founding move is a critique of Freud. Where psychoanalysis explains neurosis through the individual's childhood (ontogeny) or the species' evolution (phylogeny), Fanon insists there is a third level: sociogeny — the social structure that produces the neurosis. The Black man's alienation is not a private complex; it is manufactured by a racist society. You cannot cure it on the couch while the society keeps producing it. The cure is social transformation. For Karl: when someone's suffering is caused by the system, the answer is not therapy alone — it is changing the system. Personal pain is political evidence. 2. ## A Dying Colonialism — Key Concepts — Frantz Fanon (thematic summary) (1959) · thematic summary
A Dying Colonialism & Toward the African Revolution — Key Concepts (Frantz Fanon) Shorter works, same fire. A Dying Colonialism (1959) studies how Algeria's war of independence transformed everyday life; Toward the African Revolution (1964, posthumous) collects Fanon's political essays. Thematic summary. The veil as resistance Under French colonialism, the veil worn by Algerian women was attacked as a symbol of backwardness — the colonizer claimed to be "liberating" Algerian women by unveiling them. Fanon shows how the veil became an instrument of the revolution: women who had been unveiled by colonial pressure re-veiled themselves to carry weapons and messages past French checkpoints, invisible precisely because the occupier could not see past his own stereotype. The colonizer's gaze became the resistance's camouflage. For Karl: the oppressor's stereotypes are blind spots. The movement can move through the spaces the enemy refuses to look at. Medicine and colonialism Fanon, a psychiatrist, documented how colonial medicine was an instrument of domination: the colonized distrusted French doctors because the medical system served the occupation. During the revolution, Algerians built their own medical networks — and the very act of caring for the wounded outside the colonial system was a political education. Medicine became decolonized when the people seized it.
